## What the seminar work is

Applied AI is delivered through seminar-based teaching. The instructor guides, gives
feedback and sets the framework; **students take primary responsibility** for research,
implementation and conclusions. The work connects AI methods with a practical problem from
a real or realistic application domain.

The purpose is stated plainly (p. 1): not only to show that a model can produce a result,
but to understand **why the chosen approach is appropriate, how the result should be
evaluated, what the limitations are, and what consequences its use may have**.

After the work you should be able to:

- define precisely a problem that AI methods can address;
- identify relevant data, tools, models and existing solutions;
- select, implement or evaluate an appropriate AI approach;
- explain results with meaningful metrics and qualitative analysis;
- recognise technical, ethical, legal and organisational limitations of the solution;
- present conclusions clearly and answer questions in a professional discussion.

## Who does what

The work may be individual or in teams, depending on the instructor's guidance and the
number of students.

| Activity | Student | Instructor |
|---|---|---|
| Topic selection | Proposes or selects a problem; describes motivation, domain and expected contribution | Confirms the scope, steers towards a feasible problem, suggests literature or data |
| Literature and existing solutions review | Analyses papers, tools, models, datasets and examples from practice | Guides critical reading; requires **comparison of alternatives, not only description** |
| Practical work or case study | Implements a prototype, experiments, an evaluation or a detailed application analysis | Feedback on methodology, evaluation, scope and technical decisions |
| Presentation and defence | Presents problem, approach, results, limitations and conclusions | Leads the discussion, asks questions, assesses understanding and argumentation |

## What has to be produced

| Component | Content | Expected result | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Topic proposal | Title, motivation, problem description, application domain, work plan, expected outcome | A short proposal the instructor can approve or return for revision | 1 to 2 pages |
| Literature review | Relevant papers, technical documentation, datasets, tools and similar solutions | A critical review that explains why the selected approach is appropriate | 3 to 5 sources, more if needed |
| Prototype or analysis | Model, notebook, application, experimental pipeline, evaluation or detailed case study | A verifiable result showing what was done and what can be concluded | Depends on the topic |
| Final report | Problem, data, method, results, limitations, ethical aspects, conclusion | A document that lets another person understand and reproduce the basic workflow | 8 to 12 pages |
| Presentation | Summary of problem, approach, results, lessons learned, questions for discussion | A clear defence before the instructor and peers | Concise presentation and discussion |

The instructor may adjust the scope of a component to the type of topic.

## The semester, week by week

The schedule is indicative; final deadlines are announced by the instructor.

| Period | Work focus | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1 and 2 | Introduction to the seminar format, overview of application areas, topic selection | Short statement of interest or initial topic proposal |
| Weeks 3 and 4 | Problem definition, literature review, identifying available data | Topic proposal with work plan |
| Weeks 5 and 6 | Methodology, tool selection, evaluation design, risk definition | Short interim presentation or consultation |
| Weeks 7 to 10 | Prototype implementation, experiments, analysis or case study | Working version of the solution and preliminary results |
| Weeks 11 and 12 | Evaluation, error analysis, discussion of limitations and ethical aspects | Draft final report |
| Weeks 13 to 15 | Finalisation, presentation and defence | Final report, repository or appendices, presentation |

## Structure of the final report

1. Title, authors and abstract.
2. **Introduction and motivation:** why the problem is relevant and who would benefit.
3. **Problem definition:** inputs, outputs, limitations and assumptions.
4. **Literature and existing solutions:** what has been done and what is adopted from it.
5. **Data and tools:** data source, preparation, quality, limitations, software used.
6. **Methodology:** the selected model or approach, experimental design, evaluation metrics.
7. **Results:** quantitative and qualitative findings, with tables, charts or examples.
8. **Discussion:** errors, limitations, risks, ethical aspects, opportunities to improve.
9. **Conclusion:** what was done, what was learned, what the next step would be.
10. References and appendices: papers, datasets, code, models, prompts, other material.

## How it is assessed

Assessment reflects both the quality of execution and the quality of understanding.

| Criterion | Weight | What is looked at |
|---|---|---|
| Problem definition and work plan | 15% | Clarity of the problem, feasibility, motivation, understanding of the domain, a realistic plan |
| Literature and existing solutions review | 15% | Relevance of sources, critical comparison, connection between the literature and your own work |
| Methodology and practical implementation | 25% | Quality of the approach, soundness of the experiment, technical correctness, verifiability of results |
| Evaluation and critical analysis | 20% | Meaningful metrics, error analysis, interpretation of results, limitations and risks |
| Final report | 15% | Structure, clarity of writing, argumentation, references, quality of documentation |
| Presentation and discussion | 10% | Clear presentation, answers to questions, understanding of the decisions made, active participation |

:::warning Code alone does not pass
"Submitting code or a presentation is not sufficient; students must show that they
understand the problem, method, results, and limitations" (section 7). Almost half the
weight, 45%, sits on methodology and on evaluation with critical analysis.
:::

## Rules for using AI tools

AI tools may be allowed and encouraged when they support learning, research and
productivity, but their use **must be transparent**. You stay responsible for the accuracy,
originality, ethical soundness and understanding of what you submit.

- Allowed uses: ideas, coding assistance, explanations, summarising sources, generating
  examples, improving the language of the report.
- You may **not** submit work you do not understand or cannot explain.
- The report states **which AI tools were used and for what purpose**, especially where
  they generated code, text, experiments or analysis.
- Results obtained with AI tools must be checked, tested and critically reviewed.
- All data sources, papers, libraries and models must be properly cited.

## Minimum submission

- [ ] Final report, PDF or DOCX.
- [ ] Presentation, PPTX or PDF.
- [ ] Code, notebook or other evidence of practical work, if the topic is practical.
- [ ] Brief instructions for running the prototype, if one is submitted.
- [ ] Repository link or archive with the materials, where applicable.
- [ ] Statement on sources, data and AI tools used.

## Choosing a topic

:::tip What makes a topic good
A good Applied AI topic is not necessarily the most ambitious one. It has a clear problem,
available data or a realistic scenario, a measurable or explainable result, room for
critical analysis, and a scope feasible for one semester.
:::

Before the topic is approved you should be able to answer:

1. What specific problem am I solving?
2. Who would use the result of this work?
3. Which data, tools or models will I use?
4. How will I know whether the solution is successful?
5. What are the main limitations, risks or ethical questions?
6. What can realistically be completed by the end of the semester?

### Example topics

You may propose your own topic with the instructor's approval. It should be specific
enough to finish in one semester and substantial enough to support analysis, evaluation and
discussion.

| Area | Examples from the guidelines |
|---|---|
| Generative AI and language models | Evaluating LLMs for answering questions from course materials; a RAG prototype for university documents; comparing prompting strategies for summarising technical texts; detecting hallucinations in domain-specific answers; rules for responsible use of generative AI in education |
| Computer vision | Image classification with transfer learning; object detection for a simple industrial or security scenario; visual dataset quality and the effect of augmentation; image segmentation for a medical, urban or environmental example; classical against deep learning methods on one task |
| Predictive analytics and tabular data | Predicting student dropout risk; demand, sales or system-load forecasting; interpretable against complex models; feature importance and explainability in a business decision; anomaly detection in transactions, logs or sensor measurements |
| Recommender systems and personalisation | A simple recommender for learning resources; content-based against collaborative filtering on a public dataset; evaluating by precision, coverage, diversity and explainability; the cold-start problem; personalised learning and its ethical risks |
| AI in the public sector and society | Analysing public data on traffic, environment or urban services; a case study of an AI system in public administration; fairness analysis of a model; privacy and data protection in small AI projects; organisational readiness for an AI solution |
| MLOps and production deployment | A simple end-to-end ML pipeline; monitoring performance and detecting distribution shift; local against cloud deployment of a small prototype; documenting a model with a model card; planning tests before a pilot deployment |
