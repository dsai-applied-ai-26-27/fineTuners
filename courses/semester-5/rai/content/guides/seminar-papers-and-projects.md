## Where this comes from

Lecture 1 set out the grading: homework projects 50%, discussion and activity 20%
(presenting a paper 5 points, reading tests 5 points), exam 30%
([](lecture:01-introduction)). The details below were posted on C3 by the seminar lead,
Senka Krivić, and on the course page. The C3 copy of the introduction slides names her in
the footer; the copy first used for this site named Vahidin Hasić, who also posts
announcements.

## The two sign-ups

Both are on shared spreadsheets linked from the course page, **first come, first served**,
and were due by **18 October 2026**:

- **Project.** Form a team of 2 to 3 students, choose a project from the projects list, and
  enter all members' names next to it.
- **Paper.** Choose the paper you want to present and enter your name next to it in the
  reading list. Each student presents at least once.

If you missed the deadline, ask the seminar lead what is still open.

## Every week: read, test, discuss

- Read the assigned paper **before** the seminar and come ready to answer questions and
  take part in the discussion. Example questions are in the "How to Review Research
  Papers" guide the announcement refers to.
- For the seminar of **22 October**, two papers were set. Read **at least one** carefully:
  a short test on the paper you chose opens the seminar. You may bring a printed copy of
  the paper with your notes.
- The seminar of 15 October was cancelled ("as requested", the announcement says).

:::tip Reading for a test you may annotate
Since your annotated printout is allowed, put the answers to the usual review questions in
its margins: what problem, which method, what data, what result, what limitation. Those
are also the five parts of a presentation below, so the same notes serve both.
:::

## Giving a paper presentation

The course page's guidelines:

- **12 to 15 minutes, about 15 slides.**
- Structure:
  1. **Motivation:** why is this research important?
  2. **Problem description:** what problem does the paper address?
  3. **Methodology:** how does the proposed approach work?
  4. **Main experimental results:** what are the key findings, and what do they
     demonstrate?
  5. **Conclusions:** the main contributions, limitations, and possible future
     directions.
- Keep the slides visual: diagrams, figures, examples, no large blocks of text. Explain the
  key ideas clearly rather than every detail.
- Rehearse to stay within the time.
- **Present as if you were the paper's main author**: own the explanation, understand the
  research decisions, be ready for questions.

:::note A slide budget that fits
Not from the guidelines, one way to spend 15 slides: 1 title, 2 motivation, 2 problem,
4 method, 4 results, 2 conclusions and limitations. At about a minute a slide that lands
inside 12 to 15 minutes.
:::

## Distance-learning students

From the course information post, which is stricter than the first lecture's "attend at
least 30%":

- Present your selected paper **in person**.
- Attend **at least three in-person** classroom sessions.
- Do the paper-reading activity **during the exam session**.

## Projects

The projects are done in the teams above and count as the homework component, 50% of the
grade. **The last seminar session is the project presentations.**
